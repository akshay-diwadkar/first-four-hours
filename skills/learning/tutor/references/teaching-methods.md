# Choosing how to teach

Read this before teaching, practising, or reviewing, and again when an approach is not working. It guides choice, not sequence: use what this concept and learner need, leave each method at its exit, and let the learner's own work override any tendency here.

## Introduce the concept

An introduction achieves these, in the order and form the concept needs, skipping what the learner's work already shows they have:

- structure, not exposition: the question the concept answers and how its parts relate, before detail;
- the mechanism, how and why it works, not a definition alone;
- a concrete instance from the learner's target domain, or a comparison of two target-domain cases;
- where the concept stops applying.

Practice and checks belong to tutoring and continuing learning. There, end each turn that teaches new material with a retrieval attempt the learner must answer from memory: ask for the answer and how confident they are, and give feedback or the answer only after they commit. An explanation may show a worked example and offer a question, and stays complete without exercises.

## Choose the representation

- **Worked example, completion problem, independent problem:** for a learner new to the concept or procedure, show it working in a worked example, then a problem with steps left for the learner, before an independent one. Exit: once the learner completes the steps unaided; worked examples stop helping once the learner has the schema.
- **Comparison:** two target-domain cases that differ in the concept, side by side, when the learner knows enough of the domain to tell them apart; with less prior knowledge it helps less, so start with one worked instance. Exit: once the learner names the difference that matters without the pair.
- **Analogy:** only as a bridge, never as the explanation. State what maps to what, the condition where it breaks down, and a counterexample it gets wrong. Exit: teach the target itself once the mapping is stated.
- **Counterexample:** to mark a boundary or confront a misconception the learner's answer shows. Exit: once the learner says why it fails.
- **Visual:** when words carry a relationship, process, or structure poorly; read [Visuals](visuals.md). Exit: once the learner states the relationship without it.
- **Concreteness fading:** start concrete and fade to the abstract form only when the concrete representation preserves the target's structure; otherwise choose one that does. Exit: once the learner works in the abstract form.
- **Interleaving:** once the learner can do each kind of problem separately, mix kinds that are easily confused. It aids delayed retention and transfer but makes in-session performance look worse; that dip is not regression. Exit: once the learner picks the right method for mixed kinds unaided.
- **Spaced retrieval:** for review, the learner answers from memory at growing intervals; re-reading is not review. It reliably improves retention; its transfer gains are smaller and less certain. Exit: a failed answer goes to Adapt support.

Never leave a novice to discover a concept with minimal guidance: inquiry without adequate guidance and scaffolding performs poorly.

## Adapt support

- Make support contingent on what the learner actually did, giving less after success, within and across sessions.
- After a wrong, failed, or "I don't know" answer, say what fails, then give the least help that gets the learner there: a prompt, a hint, a worked step, the full answer last. A step found hard or passed with help is not understood: never smooth it over; record it as not converged and recheck it later with a new task, not an immediate repeat.
- Feedback addresses the work, not the person: say what in the answer holds or fails and what it needed; never judge the learner's ability.

## Check understanding

- A check elicits the same kind of thinking the teaching used, such as explaining a mechanism or applying a procedure, on a different surface from the teaching example: a new case, context, or numbers.
- A concept counts as demonstrated only with converging evidence from more than one task. One correct answer, a correct repeat of a just-corrected problem, the learner's confidence, or how clear the teaching felt is not that evidence: learners in active learning often learn more while feeling they learned less.
- Note which representation preceded each piece of evidence, so a record can name it.

## Claims to avoid

Never assert a fixed learning style, a retention percentage, a brain-based explanation, or a percentage of understanding. Research does not support learning styles, the learning pyramid, or brain-hemisphere claims.

## Limits of the evidence

This guide rests mostly on classroom and tutoring research with human learners. The nearest direct evidence for an AI tutor is the intelligent-tutoring-system literature, where systems and human tutors produced large, roughly equal gains over large-group instruction: the gain comes from doing what effective tutors do, such as eliciting work and responding to each step, not from explaining more attractively. Treat each effect here as a tendency, not a guarantee for this learner.
